Turning complex credit agreements into structured extraction rules

Result: 100+ hours of client knowledge transfer became the definitions, prompt rules and evaluation criteria that Operations, QA and Engineering built on.

Read the case study

Credit agreements spread one provision across definitions, provisos, exceptions and cross-references. The job was to decide how that language should be read and represented as data, for both Private Credit and broadly syndicated loans.

I was the main bridge between the client and our Operations, QA and Engineering teams. I wrote definitions, prompt rules and business logic, built evaluation sheets with automated sanity checks, audited research outputs, and took ambiguous cases to the client expert for a ruling.

  • Ratio testing. Testing frequency, borrower election rights, applicable baskets and exclusions.
  • Debt definition. Tracing inclusions, embedded exclusions and separate carve-outs to a final treatment.
  • Trapdoor structures. Mechanics buried in subsidiary or investment provisions, not under an obvious heading.
  • Basket sharing. How capacity is shared or reallocated across covenants without double counting.

Finding why an AI model got a covenant wrong, then fixing the rule

Result: Individual extraction failures became reusable rules, examples and validation controls instead of one-off corrections.

Read the case study

Typical failures: missing an exception, treating a carve-out as an inclusion, stopping at the main clause when a defined term held more of the answer.

Example. On a mandatory-prepayment field that needed every applicable basket, the model found one inclusion in the main clause and stopped, or returned a single basket when several applied. I traced its reasoning back to the source text, found the faulty stopping logic, added explicit rules for following defined terms and capturing all selections, gave one-shot examples, and reran the extraction to confirm the fix.

Controls added. Field validation so percentage and dropdown fields only accepted valid values. Reconciliation of manual research against a consolidated master dataset. Sanity checks that blocked data transfer when required conditions failed.

Fund Name Validator: a small tool that removed a daily bottleneck

Read the case study

Portfolio Concentration and Stressed Exposure

Finding: Ranked by outstanding balance instead of borrower count, the picture changes. Software (15.0%) and Financial Services (13.4%) lead, and about 29% and 28% of their exposure sits in stressed deals. Healthcare Services has the most borrowers but ranks seventh by exposure, with none of it stressed.

See the analysis

Question. Which industries is the portfolio most exposed to, and how much of that exposure is already under stress?

Why it matters. Credit teams cap sector exposure because a downturn in one industry hits every loan in it at once. Counting borrowers hides the dollars at risk, so exposure has to be measured by outstanding balance. Here, stressed means a deal on the watchlist, in default or restructured. The top three industries by exposure hold 40.4% of the book.

Query

SELECT b.industry,
       COUNT(DISTINCT b.borrower_id) AS borrowers,
       ROUND(SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)) AS outstanding_mm,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)
             / SUM(SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_portfolio,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(CASE WHEN d.deal_status IN ('Watchlist', 'Default', 'Restructured')
                              THEN f.outstanding_usd_mm ELSE 0 END)
             / SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm), 1) AS pct_stressed
FROM facilities f
JOIN deals d     ON d.deal_id = f.deal_id
JOIN borrowers b ON b.borrower_id = d.borrower_id
GROUP BY b.industry
ORDER BY outstanding_mm DESC;

Facility Repricing Analysis

Finding: 68 of 256 facilities now pay a different margin than at close. 45 stepped up (average +122 bps), and 40 of those sit on watchlist or defaulted deals. 23 were repriced down (average −52 bps), none of them on a stressed deal.

See the analysis

Question. Which facilities pay a different margin than when they closed, and what does the direction say about the borrower?

Why it matters. A margin step-up usually buys a waiver or amendment for a borrower under pressure. A step-down usually follows an improved credit. Repricing is an early read on where portfolio quality is moving.

Cross-check. The direction matches the credit-event history. 39 of the 45 step-ups sit on deals with a covenant waiver and amendment, and all 23 step-downs sit on deals with a repricing amendment.

DirectionFacilitiesAvg change (bps)On stressed deals
Repriced down23−520
Stepped up45+12240

Query

SELECT CASE WHEN f.current_margin_bps < f.original_margin_bps
            THEN 'Repriced down' ELSE 'Stepped up' END AS direction,
       COUNT(*) AS facilities,
       ROUND(AVG(f.current_margin_bps - f.original_margin_bps)) AS avg_change_bps,
       SUM(CASE WHEN d.deal_status IN ('Watchlist', 'Default', 'Restructured')
                THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS on_stressed_deals
FROM facilities f
JOIN deals d ON d.deal_id = f.deal_id
WHERE f.current_margin_bps <> f.original_margin_bps
GROUP BY direction;

Cross-check against credit events

SELECT ce.event_type, COUNT(DISTINCT f.facility_id) AS repriced_facilities
FROM facilities f
JOIN credit_events ce ON ce.deal_id = f.deal_id
WHERE f.current_margin_bps <> f.original_margin_bps
  AND ce.event_type IN ('Repricing Amendment', 'Covenant Waiver & Amendment')
GROUP BY ce.event_type;

Maturity Wall Analysis

Finding: 2028 is the largest wall at $16.1B (32% of outstanding), and 46% of the book matures by the end of 2028. The stressed borrowers peak later: $3.2B in 2030, 28% of that year’s maturities.

See the analysis

Question. How much debt matures each year, and where is refinancing pressure most likely to build?

Why it matters. Large maturity concentrations create liquidity and refinancing risk, but the size of a wall matters less than who has to refinance. A big year of performing borrowers is a different problem from a smaller year weighted toward watchlist names. 27 of the 256 facilities have already had their maturity extended, which has pushed part of the wall out.

Query

SELECT strftime('%Y', f.maturity_date) AS maturity_year,
       COUNT(*) AS facilities,
       ROUND(SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)) AS outstanding_mm,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)
             / SUM(SUM(f.outstanding_usd_mm)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_of_total,
       ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN d.deal_status IN ('Watchlist', 'Default', 'Restructured')
                      THEN f.outstanding_usd_mm ELSE 0 END)) AS stressed_mm
FROM facilities f
JOIN deals d ON d.deal_id = f.deal_id
WHERE f.outstanding_usd_mm > 0
GROUP BY maturity_year
ORDER BY maturity_year;

Covenant Headroom Early Warning

Finding: At the latest test, all 8 covenant failures sit on defaulted deals. 14 of 21 watchlist tests have under 10% headroom, while 65 of 66 performing tests have more than 20%.

See the analysis

Question. Which borrowers are closest to breaching a financial covenant, and does thin headroom line up with the credits we already worry about?

Why it matters. Headroom is the distance between a borrower’s actual ratio and the covenant level. Monitoring teams review it every quarter because it shows deterioration before a breach, waiver or default.

Reading the result. A 20% headroom screen separates stressed from performing credits cleanly in this dataset. Real data is noisier, so in practice I would track the quarter-on-quarter trend as well as the level. The table covers the latest test of each covenant and excludes springing covenants that weren’t tested.

Deal statusTestsFailedUnder 10%10 to 20%Over 20%Lowest headroom
Default88800-102.1%
Watchlist2101461-9.1%
Restructured2000244.0%
Performing660016518.9%
Repaid3000333.5%

Query

SELECT d.deal_status,
       COUNT(*) AS tests,
       SUM(t.test_result = 'Fail') AS failed,
       SUM(t.headroom_pct < 10) AS under_10,
       SUM(t.headroom_pct >= 10 AND t.headroom_pct < 20) AS from_10_to_20,
       SUM(t.headroom_pct >= 20) AS over_20,
       ROUND(MIN(t.headroom_pct), 1) AS lowest_headroom_pct
FROM covenant_tests t
JOIN deals d ON d.deal_id = t.deal_id
WHERE (t.fin_cov_id, t.period_end) IN
      (SELECT fin_cov_id, MAX(period_end)
       FROM covenant_tests GROUP BY fin_cov_id)
  AND t.headroom_pct IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY d.deal_status
ORDER BY CASE d.deal_status WHEN 'Default' THEN 1 WHEN 'Watchlist' THEN 2
                            WHEN 'Restructured' THEN 3 WHEN 'Performing' THEN 4 ELSE 5 END;

Lender Exposure to Stressed Credits

Finding: Banks hold the most dollars but the least stress: 10.9% of their commitments are in stressed deals, spread across about 39 borrowers each. BDCs are at 29.5% and direct lenders at 21.9%, each lending to far fewer borrowers (11 and 14 on average).

See the analysis

Question. Which types of lender carry the most exposure to stressed credits, and how diversified are they?

Why it matters. When a deal moves to the watchlist, every lender in the syndicate is exposed, but not equally. A lender’s facility count says little about diversification. What matters is how many different borrowers it lends to and how much of its book is already stressed. At the single-lender level the most exposed are Copperleaf Direct Lending (35.9%) and Ashford Lane Capital BDC (34.4%). Exposure here is measured on commitments, not funded balances.

Query

WITH lender_stress AS (
  SELECT fa.lender_id,
         COUNT(DISTINCT d.borrower_id) AS borrowers,
         SUM(fa.commitment_usd_mm) AS total_mm,
         SUM(CASE WHEN d.deal_status IN ('Watchlist', 'Default', 'Restructured')
                  THEN fa.commitment_usd_mm ELSE 0 END) AS stressed_mm
  FROM facility_allocations fa
  JOIN facilities f ON f.facility_id = fa.facility_id
  JOIN deals d      ON d.deal_id = f.deal_id
  GROUP BY fa.lender_id
)
SELECT l.lender_type,
       COUNT(*) AS lenders,
       ROUND(AVG(s.borrowers), 1) AS avg_borrowers,
       ROUND(SUM(s.total_mm)) AS commitments_mm,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(s.stressed_mm) / SUM(s.total_mm), 1) AS pct_stressed
FROM lender_stress s
JOIN lenders l ON l.lender_id = s.lender_id
GROUP BY l.lender_type
ORDER BY pct_stressed DESC;
